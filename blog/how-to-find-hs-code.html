How to Find Your HS Code Without a Customs Broker (2026 Guide)

Your HS code determines your tariff rate. Getting it wrong can mean overpaying duties for years — or triggering a Customs audit. Here's how to look it up correctly in about 60 seconds.

Every product imported into the United States is assigned a 10-digit Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code. That code controls everything: what duty rate you pay, whether Section 301 tariffs apply, whether you qualify for trade agreement preferences like USMCA, and what CBP is looking for when your shipment arrives at the port.

Most importers either ask their supplier for the code (unreliable — your supplier uses their country's classification, which differs after the first 6 digits) or pay a customs broker every time (fine for complex products, overkill for straightforward ones). Here's how to do it yourself.

First: understand what an HS code actually is

The HS code has a hierarchy. Each level narrows down the classification:

85 23 90 . 40 00
Chapter (2 digits) — broad category: "Electrical machinery"
Heading (4 digits) — product type: "Monitors and displays"
Subheading (6 digits) — international standard breakout
US statistical suffix (digits 7–8) — US-specific split
US statistical suffix (digits 9–10) — further US detail

The first 6 digits are the same worldwide. Your Chinese supplier's 6-digit code should match the US HTS code at the same level — useful as a cross-check, but don't rely on digits 7–10 from your supplier.

The 60-second method: USITC HTS Search

The official US lookup tool is hts.usitc.gov. It's free, official, and searchable. Here's the fastest path:

Step 1

Go to hts.usitc.gov and search your product

Type 2–4 descriptive words about your product. Be specific about material and function. "Bluetooth speaker" is better than "electronics." "Polyester fleece jacket, men's" is better than "jacket."

Step 2

Browse the results and check Chapter notes

USITC returns candidate headings. Click through to the Chapter and read the Chapter Notes — these are legally binding definitions that often override common-sense product names. A "case for smartphones" might classify under Chapter 42 (leather goods) rather than Chapter 85 (electronics), depending on material.

Step 3

Find the 10-digit HTS code and confirm the duty rate

The full 10-digit code will show the general duty rate, any applicable Section 232 or Section 301 tariffs, and trade agreement columns (USMCA, etc.). Confirm the description matches your product precisely — "woven" vs "knit" matters, "AC" vs "DC" motors matter, made of plastic vs rubber matters.

Step 4

Cross-check with your supplier's code

Ask your supplier for their HS code. Compare the first 6 digits to your US HTS code. They should match. If they don't, one of you has a classification issue — or your product genuinely classifies differently in each country's extended system, which is possible but uncommon.

When to get a binding ruling from CBP

If your product is genuinely ambiguous, or if the duty difference between two possible classifications is large enough to matter at scale, request a Binding Ruling from US Customs and Border Protection (cbp.gov/trade). A binding ruling is CBP's official written determination of your product's correct HTS classification. It's legally binding on CBP and protects you from duty reclassification on future shipments of the same product.

Binding rulings are free and typically take 30–60 days. They're worth requesting if: your annual import value is $200K+, the classification is contested or non-obvious, or if you're making a significant sourcing decision based on the duty rate.

Frequently asked questions

What is an HS code and why does it matter?

An HS (Harmonized System) code is a 6-digit international product classification number. For US imports, this extends to 10 digits as an HTS code. Your code determines your import duty rate, any additional tariffs (like Section 301 duties on Chinese goods), and your compliance obligations. Wrong code = wrong duty rate — either over- or underpaid, both of which create problems.

Where can I look up my HS code for free?

The official source is hts.usitc.gov. Type keywords about your product, browse the chapter structure, and confirm your code at the 10-digit level. SourcingLens also offers AI-assisted classification via our free landed cost tool — paste a product description and it returns candidate HTS codes with duty rates.

What's the difference between HS, HTS, and Schedule B?

HS code (6 digits): the international standard used worldwide. HTS code (10 digits): US-specific extension for imports. Schedule B (10 digits): US export classification. If you're importing into the US, you need the HTS code.

Can I use my supplier's HS code?

Only as a starting point. Your supplier's code uses their country's extended digits (7–10), which differ from the US HTS. The first 6 digits should match — use those to cross-check. Never copy digits 7–10 from a supplier's commercial invoice as your US HTS code.

What happens if I use the wrong HS code?

Underpaying duties triggers Customs audit, back-duty assessment, and potential penalties up to 4x the unpaid duty. Overpaying means you've lost real money for every shipment. If you're genuinely uncertain, consult a licensed customs broker or request a binding ruling from CBP — it's free and legally protects you.
