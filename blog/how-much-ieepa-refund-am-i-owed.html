How Much IEEPA Tariff Refund Am I Owed? Real Examples for Small Businesses (2026)

📅 June 2026 ⏱ 7 min read ✍️ SourcingLens

The most common question importers ask after hearing about IEEPA tariff refunds is simple: how much am I actually going to get back?

The answer depends on four variables: what you imported, where it came from, how much duty you paid, and when you paid it. This article walks through the calculation with real product examples — and shows you how to estimate your number without a customs broker.

The refund formula

Your IEEPA refund is the IEEPA duty you paid, plus statutory interest at 6% per year from payment date to refund date.

Refund = IEEPA duty paid + (IEEPA duty × 6% × days held / 365)

Example: $10,000 duty paid Oct 2025 → refund ~Sep 2026 = 335 days
Interest = $10,000 × 6% × (335/365) = $551
Total refund = $10,000 + $551 = $10,551

Note on interest rate: CBP applies a 6% annual interest rate for corporate importers. The rate is set by statute and does not vary with market conditions. It compounds from your original duty payment date to the date CBP processes your reliquidation.

What counts as IEEPA duty — and what doesn't

This is the most important distinction. Your Form 7501 entry summary typically shows multiple duty lines. Only the IEEPA portion is refundable.

China importers — read carefully: If you import from China, you likely paid both Section 301 tariffs (not refundable) and IEEPA tariffs (refundable) on top of the base MFN rate. Only the IEEPA-specific duty lines are being refunded — not the 25% Section 301 tariff you've been paying since 2018.

Key insight: The refund is calculated on the IEEPA-specific duty only, not your total landed cost. For China imports in 2025, IEEPA duties were layered on top of existing Section 301 tariffs — so your 7501 may show 145% total additional duties but only the IEEPA portion (above the pre-existing Section 301 rate) is refundable.

How to find your actual IEEPA duty amount

The most reliable source is your CBP Form 7501 Entry Summary. Look at Box 27 (HTS classification) for any lines beginning with 9903.01 or 9903.08. The associated duty amounts in the adjacent columns are your IEEPA duties.

If you can access your ACE portal, export your entry history — it includes a breakdown by tariff line that makes this calculation straightforward.

When will the money actually arrive?

Importers who submitted CAPE Declarations in late April and May 2026 are reporting ACH deposits hitting their accounts in June and July. The pipeline is moving. If you haven't filed yet, submitting now means your refund could arrive before end of Q3 2026.

Should I hire a customs broker for this?

For most small and mid-sized importers, you don't need to. The CAPE process is designed for self-service by the IOR. If you have fewer than 100 entries, you can likely handle it in an afternoon with the right preparation. If you have hundreds or thousands of entries, a broker's time may be worth paying for — but the refund economics still strongly favor doing it yourself or with minimal assistance.

The most common reason importers hire brokers for this is ACE portal access — many small importers don't have their own ACE account and it was all handled by their broker. In that case, asking the broker to file CAPE on your behalf is the simplest path.
