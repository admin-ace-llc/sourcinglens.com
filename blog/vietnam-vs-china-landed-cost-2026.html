Vietnam vs China: Real Landed Cost Comparison for US Importers (2026)

The tariff gap between China and Vietnam is now 20–25 percentage points. Here's what that means in dollars — and how to decide if a switch makes sense for your products.

In 2022, "China plus one" was a hedge strategy for large enterprises. In 2026, it's a survival calculation for small and mid-size importers. Section 301 tariffs have changed the landed cost math so dramatically that many products that made sense to source from China two years ago simply don't anymore — at least not without a rigorous comparison.

This article gives you that comparison, with real numbers.

The core issue: a 20–25 point tariff gap

Vietnamese goods entering the US face MFN (Most Favored Nation) duty rates of 0–6% for most manufactured goods. Chinese goods face the same MFN rates, plus Section 301 additional duties of 7.5–145%, with most categories landing at 25%.

That gap — roughly 20–25 percentage points on CIF value — is the central fact of the 2026 import landscape. On a $500,000 annual import order, that's $100,000–$125,000 in additional annual cost from China that simply doesn't apply to Vietnam.

When China still wins

Vietnam isn't the right call for every product. China retains a clear advantage in several situations:

  • Complex electronics requiring deep supply chain integration — component sourcing, specialized tooling, and tight tolerances are still easier to manage in China's Shenzhen/Dongguan clusters
  • Very low MOQs — Vietnamese factories often require larger minimum orders, making China better for smaller import volumes
  • Products with low Section 301 exposure — if your China tariff is only 7.5%, the math is closer and China's supply chain advantages may tip the balance
  • Speed-to-market — China's deeper supplier network means faster sampling, tooling, and ramp-up for new products
  • High supply chain complexity — products requiring many sub-components still benefit from China's proximity to raw materials and parts

When Vietnam wins

Vietnam is the stronger choice when:

  • Your product faces 25%+ Section 301 tariffs from China (most electronics, consumer goods, furniture)
  • You're importing at volumes above 5,000–10,000 units/year (where FOB price differentials are negotiable)
  • Your product has a well-established Vietnamese manufacturing ecosystem (footwear, apparel, furniture, electronics assembly)
  • You're planning 12+ months ahead and have time to qualify a new supplier
  • Your China supplier is itself using Vietnamese sub-components (common now — origin rules matter)

What about Mexico and India?

Mexico deserves serious consideration for importers with US West Coast or Midwest distribution. USMCA preferential rates are 0% for qualifying goods, transit time is 3–7 days by truck, and nearshoring removes ocean freight entirely. FOB prices are typically 20–35% above equivalent China suppliers, but total landed cost is competitive for many categories once tariffs and freight are included.

India is the emerging third option — MFN rates of 0–7.5%, growing manufacturing capacity in consumer goods, textiles, and increasingly electronics assembly, with a large English-speaking workforce. Lead times are similar to Vietnam (4–6 weeks sea freight to US). For importers diversifying away from China-Vietnam concentration, India adds geographic resilience.

Bottom line

For most US importers sourcing manufactured goods from China at volumes above $100K/year, a Vietnam comparison is worth running before your next contract renewal. The tariff gap alone is typically worth $15,000–$50,000+ per year on mid-size import programs. The FOB price premium and supplier qualification costs are real but usually lower than the tariff savings.

Frequently asked questions

Is manufacturing in Vietnam cheaper than China in 2026?

On a landed cost basis for US importers, yes — typically 15–30% cheaper for most manufactured goods, primarily due to the Section 301 tariff gap. Vietnam's FOB prices are often 5–15% higher than China, but the 20–25 point tariff advantage more than offsets this in most product categories.

What are the main risks of switching from China to Vietnam?

The primary risks are: (1) supplier qualification time (3–9 months), (2) higher MOQ requirements, (3) less depth in the supplier ecosystem for complex products, (4) potential quality variability during ramp-up, and (5) origin scrutiny — some Chinese factories are "washing" goods through Vietnam, which carries legal risk. Always verify actual manufacturing origin.

How long does it take to switch from China to Vietnam?

Typically 3–9 months end-to-end: supplier identification and qualification (1–2 months), sampling and quality validation (1–2 months), tooling and production setup (1–2 months), then first commercial run. Allow more time for complex products or those requiring regulatory certifications.

What products make the most sense to move from China to Vietnam?

Best candidates: consumer electronics and tech accessories, furniture and home goods, footwear and apparel, plastic consumer products, and light industrial components. These categories have well-developed Vietnamese supply chains and face the highest Section 301 exposure from China.
